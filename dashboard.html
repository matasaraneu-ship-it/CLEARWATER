Clearwater — how three anomaly signals were built, and where they land

HHI filters the whole dataset down to 190 contracts sitting inside 15 highly-concentrated markets. Everything below happens inside that filtered population — who avoided competition and how, and whose contracts grew the most after award.

Risk indicators, not findings. Every number below reflects public USASpending.gov data — not evidence of wrongdoing. Real vendor and agency names are shown because this is real public procurement data.

Signal 2 — who avoided real competition, and how

Two ways a contract can skip real competition

Within the 190: Group A — the solicitation was legally open, but only one vendor bid. Group B — the government didn't open it to competition at all, under a FAR-justified exception. These are different phenomena with different "who benefits" stories, so they're not merged into one flag.

Group B — the actual legal reason ("why")

From the FAR justification code on file for each award

What "why" means

Pulled straight from each award's FAR citation, not inferred:

Authorized by statute (32, 48%) — FAR 6.302-5: Congress requires the source, not agency discretion. Covers 8(a), HUBZone, WOSB, veteran-owned, and AbilityOne set-asides.
Only one source (23, 35%) — FAR 6.302-1: agency judgment that only one vendor can meet the need. The category actually worth scrutinizing case-by-case.
SAP non-competition (8, 12%) — small dollar value, competition not required by rule.
National security / brand name / follow-on (3, 5%) — narrow, specific exceptions.
So what this signal actually shows: "not competed" is mostly not a judgment call by the contracting officer — nearly half (48%) is Congress mandating the source via small-business and set-aside statutes, which is policy working as intended, not a risk indicator. The 35% under FAR 6.302-1 ("only one source") is where agency discretion is actually in play, and Group A — open competition that still drew one bidder — is arguably the more interesting pattern, since nothing forced that outcome. Carahsoft (3x, across 3 different agencies) and the Parsons/Planned Systems/Intellidyne pairs are the names worth a closer look, though a reseller winning repeat sole-bidder software awards has an ordinary explanation (exclusive distribution rights) worth checking before reading more into it.

Key finding: growth is the norm, not the exception — 78% of these 178 contracts obligated more than their original award, 65% at least doubled, median growth 2.69×. The interesting question isn't "did it grow" (nearly all did), it's who grew the most and how often — which the leaderboards below answer directly.

Does it matter whether the contract was competed?

Same 178 ratios, split by extent_competed_description: Competed (full & open, exclusion-of-sources, or SAP-with-competition — 116 contracts, includes Group A's 1-bid awards) vs. Not competed (Group B's 62 with a valid ratio).

Ratio distribution, competed vs. not competed

Bars show % of each group, not raw counts — group sizes differ (116 vs. 62)

Competed (n=116)
Not competed (n=62)
Key finding: the two distributions are almost indistinguishable in the middle — medians (2.68× vs. 2.76×) and the share that grew at all (78% vs. 77%) are within noise of each other. The real difference is entirely in the tail: the three most extreme blowouts in the whole dataset — Leidos at 364× (Defense Health Agency), Amentum at 47× (Missile Defense Agency), ManTech at 25× (Defense Health Agency) — are all in competed awards, mostly large IDIQ task orders where the "competition" happened once at the vehicle level, years before the ballooning. Not competed's worst case tops out at 15.85×. So competition status alone doesn't predict or protect against ballooning — consistent with the earlier finding that these are independent signals, not one underlying pattern.

Vendor leaderboards

Across all 190 — who does this repeatedly, and by how much

Two different questions: how often does a vendor's contract grow at all, and — weighted by dollars, not just counting contracts — how much did a vendor's whole book of business in this population grow in total.

Times ballooned (ratio > 1×), min. 2 contracts — 16 qualify

★ = ballooned ≥2 times, out of at least 2 total contracts

Aggregate nominal→total ratio, min. 2 contracts & $1M base — 22 qualify

★ = Σtotal÷Σbase > 1× — real dollar-weighted growth, not just eligible

Reading the two side by side: Johns Hopkins APL ballooned on all 5 of its contracts (8.49× in aggregate dollars) — the most consistent pattern in the data. Leidos stands out differently: only a 4.86× aggregate ratio, but $156.4M → $760.0M is a $603.6M nominal increase, the largest dollar swing of any vendor here, on just 4 contracts. Frequency and dollar magnitude tell different stories — worth showing both rather than picking one "worst" list. These same blue/green markers carry into the company rollup in Step 5.
